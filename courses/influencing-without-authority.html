01Authority vs. Influence

What you will learn

  • Distinguish formal decision rights from the ability to move a decision
  • Explain how authority changes the behavior, result, or engineering decision in a concrete example
  • Connect authority with influence and predict what changes when one assumption changes
  • Verify the idea with a normal case and an important edge, failure, or scale case

Explanation

Authority is a formal right to decide -- a tech lead can approve a design, a manager can approve a hire.

Key concepts

authorityinfluencedecision rightsindividual contributor influence

Example

# the distinction this whole course rests on:
# authority: 'I can approve this' -- a formal right, held by a specific role
# influence: 'I can help the person who approves this make a better decision'
# -- available to anyone, regardless of title, and the actual subject of this course
Common mistake: Assuming influence requires becoming someone's manager first -- influence is available to any engineer immediately, built through credibility and how well you help the actual decision owner decide, not through acquiring formal authority.
02Credibility and Trust

What you will learn

  • Explain how credibility is built and lost before any single conversation happens
  • Explain how credibility changes the behavior, result, or engineering decision in a concrete example
  • Connect credibility with trust and predict what changes when one assumption changes
  • Verify the idea with a normal case and an important edge, failure, or scale case

Explanation

Credibility is built cumulatively, before the specific conversation where you need it -- through a track record of being accurate (your claims turn out to be true), being fair (you represent other viewpoints honestly, not just your own case), and being consistent (you don't shift your position based on who's in the room).

Key concepts

credibilitytrusttrack recordconsistency

Example

# why identical proposals land differently:
# engineer A: has a track record of being right about risk estimates, and
# openly flagged their OWN proposal's downsides last time -- the room extends trust
# engineer B: is new, or has previously overstated a benefit that didn't pan out --
# the SAME proposal gets more scrutiny, not because of the idea's merit
Common mistake: Treating credibility as something you can manufacture in the moment through confident delivery -- credibility is built over many prior interactions being accurate and fair, not created fresh.
03Understanding Decision Rights

  • Connect decision owner with decision rights and predict what changes when one assumption changes
  • Verify the idea with a normal case and an important edge, failure, or scale case

Explanation

Before trying to influence a decision, you need to know who actually owns it -- confusing an interested party for the decision owner wastes effort convincing the wrong person, or worse, makes the real owner feel bypassed.

Key concepts

decision ownerdecision rightsidentifying the real approver

Example

# a common, avoidable mistake:
# spending a week convincing a skeptical peer engineer, only to learn
# the ACTUAL decision owner is the tech lead, who was never part of the conversation
# -- all that effort went toward someone who was never going to make the call
Common mistake: Assuming the most vocal or most senior person in the room is automatically the decision owner -- decision rights depend on the actual structure of a specific decision, which isn't always the same as seniority or how loudly someone objects.
04Stakeholder Mapping

What you will learn

  • Map decision role, engagement requirement, and stance as three independent dimensions for a real change
  • Explain how stakeholder mapping changes the behavior, result, or engineering decision in a concrete example
  • Connect stakeholder mapping with decision role and predict what changes when one assumption changes
  • Verify the idea with a normal case and an important edge, failure, or scale case

Explanation

Stakeholder mapping means identifying, for everyone who matters to a decision, three genuinely separate things: their decision role (do they own the decision, advise on it, or have no formal right at all), their engagement requirement (do they need to collaborate, be consulted, or just be informed), and their current stance (are they a supporter, neutral.

Key concepts

stakeholder mappingdecision roleengagement requirementstance

Example

# three independent dimensions, not one combined label:
# a VP might have NO formal decision right on this specific technical call,
# but be an influential SUPPORTER whose backing changes how seriously it's taken
# the actual decision owner (a tech lead) might currently be NEUTRAL -- undecided,
# not yet a blocker, but not yet convinced either
Common mistake: Collapsing decision role, engagement need, and current stance into one exclusive label like 'blocker' or 'supporter' -- this loses the genuinely useful information that someone can have influence without formal authority, or hold formal authority while still being undecided.

Every stakeholder is operating under their own incentives and constraints -- a PM protecting a quarterly deadline, a security team protecting against a specific class of past incident, an adjacent team protecting their own already-committed roadmap.

Key concepts

incentivesconstraintscompeting prioritiesreasonable disagreement

Example

# your proposal is technically sound, but the PM just committed this quarter's

06Listening Before Persuading

What you will learn

  • Explain how genuine listening can change the proposal itself, not just how it's delivered
  • Explain how genuine listening changes the behavior, result, or engineering decision in a concrete example
  • Connect genuine listening with revising the proposal and predict what changes when one assumption changes
  • Verify the idea with a normal case and an important edge, failure, or scale case

Explanation

Listening before persuading isn't a delivery technique to make people feel heard before you convince them anyway -- done genuinely, it can change the actual proposal. A stakeholder's objection often contains real information (a constraint, a past incident, a dependency) that should legitimately change what you're proposing, not just how you pitch it.

Key concepts

genuine listeningrevising the proposalnot a scripted technique

Example

# the real test of genuine listening:
# NOT: nodding along, then delivering the identical pitch anyway
# BUT: 'I hadn't considered that this migration would break your team's existing
# monitoring -- let me revise the plan to include a monitoring migration step first'
# -- the PROPOSAL itself changed because of what you heard, not just your delivery
Common mistake: Treating 'listen first' as a rhetorical technique to perform before delivering an unchanged pitch -- genuine listening means being willing to actually revise the proposal based on new information, not just appearing receptive before repeating yourself.
07Framing for Different Audiences

What you will learn

  • Adapt the same technical fact for different audiences without changing or hiding the fact itself
  • Explain how audience framing changes the behavior, result, or engineering decision in a concrete example
  • Connect audience framing with emphasis vs. truth and predict what changes when one assumption changes
  • Verify the idea with a normal case and an important edge, failure, or scale case

Explanation

Framing means presenting the same underlying facts in the way that addresses a specific audience's actual concern -- an engineer wants to know how it works and what could break; a product manager wants to know user and timeline impact; an executive wants risk and cost at a glance; a customer wants to know what changes for them.

Key concepts

audience framingemphasis vs. truthhonest framing

Example

# same fact, appropriately framed for different audiences -- fact UNCHANGED:
# engineer: 'this requires a 4-hour maintenance window due to a schema lock'
# executive: 'this requires 4 hours of planned downtime; alternative is 3x
# the timeline to avoid downtime entirely -- recommend accepting the downtime'
# BOTH state the same 4-hour downtime fact -- neither hides or shrinks it
Common mistake: Softening or omitting a real risk/cost to make a proposal land better with a specific audience -- this crosses from framing into misleading, and is explicitly one of the things this course.
08Evidence, Experiments and Prototypes

What you will learn

  • Match evidence type to the strength of claim being made, without treating any one type as universally best
  • Explain how proportionate evidence changes the behavior, result, or engineering decision in a concrete example
  • Connect proportionate evidence with experiments and predict what changes when one assumption changes
  • Verify the idea with a normal case and an important edge, failure, or scale case

Explanation

Different claims warrant different evidence -- a low-risk local code change might reasonably be justified by a quick prototype; a performance claim is better supported by actual metrics or a controlled experiment than by opinion; a user-experience claim benefits from real user research; a reliability concern is often best supported by incident history.

Key concepts

proportionate evidenceexperimentsprototypesclaim-to-evidence matching

Example

# matching evidence to the CLAIM, not one universal ranking:
# 'let's rename this internal variable' -> a quick local check is proportionate;
# a full controlled experiment would be genuine overkill here
# 'let's rewrite our core data pipeline' -> a prototype or experiment is proportionate;
# a single engineer's opinion is NOT enough evidence for a costly, hard-to-reverse choice
Common mistake: Assuming one evidence type (e.g. 'always run an experiment') is universally the strongest choice -- the right evidence depends on the claim's size, cost, and reversibility; overkill evidence for a trivial change wastes time just as much as insufficient evidence for a big one undersells the risk.
09Writing Persuasive Technical Proposals

What you will learn

  • Structure a written technical proposal that anticipates objections rather than only stating the idea
  • Explain how persuasive writing changes the behavior, result, or engineering decision in a concrete example
  • Connect persuasive writing with anticipating objections and predict what changes when one assumption changes
  • Verify the idea with a normal case and an important edge, failure, or scale case

Explanation

A persuasive technical proposal does more than describe the idea -- it states the problem in terms the reader already cares about, presents evidence proportionate to the claim, and explicitly anticipates and addresses likely objections before the reader has to raise them.

Key concepts

persuasive writinganticipating objectionsproposal structure

Example

# what an unaddressed objection actually costs you:
# a proposal that says 'we should migrate to X' but never mentions the migration
# risk or cost -- the reader is left silently thinking 'but what if this breaks
# production during the migration?' with no answer given -- and that unaddressed
# thought is often exactly what turns into a quiet, unstated 'no'
Common mistake: Writing a proposal that only describes the desired outcome, leaving cost, risk, and likely objections for the reader to raise (or silently hold against it) -- addressing these proactively in.
10Influencing in Meetings and Design Reviews

What you will learn

  • Prepare for and participate in a design review in a way that actually helps move a decision
  • Explain how design review preparation changes the behavior, result, or engineering decision in a concrete example
  • Connect design review preparation with inviting disagreement and predict what changes when one assumption changes
  • Verify the idea with a normal case and an important edge, failure, or scale case

Explanation

A design review is where a proposal gets tested in real time -- preparing means anticipating the specific objections THIS specific audience is likely to raise (not a generic list), and having a clear, honest answer ready, including 'I don't know yet, here's how I'd find out' where genuinely true.

Key concepts

design review preparationinviting disagreementsurfacing objections early

Example

# actively inviting disagreement, rather than just defending:
# 'before I walk through the proposal, what's the concern you'd expect someone
# to raise about this approach?' -- this surfaces the REAL objection early,
# while it's still cheap to address, instead of discovering it after the room
# has already quietly decided against the idea
Common mistake: Treating a design review purely as a presentation to defend, rather than a conversation to actively invite pushback in -- objections surfaced early are far cheaper to address than ones that.
11Handling Objections Without Defensiveness

What you will learn

  • Respond to a raised objection by acknowledging, clarifying, and offering evidence rather than becoming defensive
  • Explain how acknowledging concerns changes the behavior, result, or engineering decision in a concrete example
  • Connect acknowledging concerns with clarifying assumptions and predict what changes when one assumption changes
  • Verify the idea with a normal case and an important edge, failure, or scale case

Explanation

A defensive response to an objection ('that's not going to happen' or immediately arguing back) tends to escalate rather than resolve, and damages the credibility the whole rest of this course depends on.

Key concepts

acknowledging concernsclarifying assumptionsavoiding defensiveness

Example

# defensive: 'no it's not, I've already tested this' (dismisses the concern outright)

# different this time, and here's the rollback plan if I'm wrong'
# -- acknowledges, clarifies the assumption, and offers evidence/options
Common mistake: Responding to an objection by immediately arguing back or dismissing it -- this tends to escalate the disagreement and damages credibility, even when the underlying technical point is.
12Productive Disagreement and Conflict

What you will learn

  • Distinguish disagreement from disrespect, and disagree while preserving the working relationship
  • Explain how disagreement vs. disrespect changes the behavior, result, or engineering decision in a concrete example
  • Connect disagreement vs. disrespect with psychological safety and predict what changes when one assumption changes
  • Verify the idea with a normal case and an important edge, failure, or scale case

Explanation

Disagreement is a normal, healthy part of technical work -- two reasonable engineers can look at the same facts and reach different conclusions, and treating disagreement itself as a problem to eliminate (rather than something to work through) tends to suppress genuinely useful pushback.

Key concepts

disagreement vs. disrespectpsychological safetypreserving the relationship

Example

# disagreement (healthy) vs. disrespect (damaging):
# disagreement: 'I see this differently -- I think the caching layer adds more
# risk than it saves here, and here's why'
# disrespect: 'that's a naive suggestion' or refusing to explain why you disagree
# -- the first invites continued discussion; the second shuts it down and damages
# trust needed for future disagreements to go well
Common mistake: Treating disagreement itself as something to avoid or suppress -- healthy technical disagreement, handled respectfully, is normal and valuable; the goal is disagreeing well, not avoiding.
13Negotiating Scope, Deadlines and Technical Debt

What you will learn

  • Negotiate trade-offs honestly, recognizing that not every constraint can be maximized simultaneously
  • Explain how trade-off negotiation changes the behavior, result, or engineering decision in a concrete example
  • Connect trade-off negotiation with coupled constraints and predict what changes when one assumption changes
  • Verify the idea with a normal case and an important edge, failure, or scale case

Explanation

Delivery date, feature scope, reliability, and technical debt are genuinely coupled -- you cannot maximize all of them simultaneously with fixed capacity, and pretending otherwise (agreeing to an unrealistic combination just to avoid conflict) just moves the actual failure to a worse, later moment.

Key concepts

trade-off negotiationcoupled constraintshonest infeasibility

Example

# the trade-off, made visible instead of quietly ignored:
# 'ship all this scope, hit this date, AND maintain full reliability, with no
# extra staffing' is often not simultaneously achievable -- naming this honestly
# ('something has to give: less scope, more time, more people, or accepted risk')
# is more useful than agreeing to an impossible combination and failing later
Common mistake: Agreeing to an unrealistic combination of scope, date, and reliability just to avoid a difficult conversation now -- this doesn't resolve the actual constraint, it just delays the failure to a later, usually worse moment.
14Coalition Building and Cross-Functional Alignment

What you will learn

  • Build support across teams before a decision point, rather than only at the moment of decision
  • Explain how coalition building changes the behavior, result, or engineering decision in a concrete example
  • Connect coalition building with pre-alignment and predict what changes when one assumption changes
  • Verify the idea with a normal case and an important edge, failure, or scale case

Explanation

Coalition building means talking to key stakeholders individually BEFORE a decision meeting, understanding their specific concerns, and where possible addressing them ahead of time -- so the actual decision meeting is confirming alignment that already exists, rather than the first time anyone has heard the proposal or raised their concerns.

Key concepts

coalition buildingpre-alignmentgenuine incorporation of feedback

Example

# genuine coalition building vs. manufactured consensus:
# genuine: talking to the security team a week before the review, learning their
# specific concern, and revising the proposal to address it BEFORE the meeting
# manufactured (NOT what this teaches): privately pressuring people to publicly
# agree regardless of their actual view, so the meeting just looks unanimous
Common mistake: Confusing genuine coalition building (incorporating real feedback ahead of time) with manufacturing artificial consensus (pressuring people to appear aligned regardless of their actual view).
15Ethical Influence and a Repeatable Process

What you will learn

  • Synthesize the course into one repeatable process, stated honestly as improving decision quality, not guaranteeing agreement
  • Explain how repeatable influence process changes the behavior, result, or engineering decision in a concrete example
  • Connect repeatable influence process with improving decision quality and predict what changes when one assumption changes
  • Verify the idea with a normal case and an important edge, failure, or scale case

Explanation

Every idea in this course fits into one repeatable process: (1) clarify the decision, (2) identify the decision owner, (3) map stakeholders, (4) listen for interests and constraints, (5) gather proportionate evidence, (6) frame the proposal honestly, (7) invite objections, (8) negotiate trade-offs.

Key concepts

repeatable influence processimproving decision qualityaccepting a legitimate no

Example

# the repeatable process, and its honest limit:
# clarify -> identify owner -> map stakeholders -> listen -> gather evidence ->
# frame honestly -> invite objections -> negotiate -> escalate only if legitimate
# -> accept the final decision when appropriate
# this process improves decision QUALITY -- it does not guarantee AGREEMENT,
# and persisting past a legitimate 'no' is not a version of doing this well
Common mistake: Treating a well-executed influence process as something that should always end in agreement -- a legitimate decision owner can hear a well-framed, well-evidenced case and still reasonably.
