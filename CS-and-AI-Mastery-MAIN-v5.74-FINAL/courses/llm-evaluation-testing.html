02Defining Goals and Success Criteria

What you will learn

  • Convert a vague goal into testable acceptance criteria and identify failure categories
  • Explain how testable success criteria changes the behavior, result, or engineering decision in a concrete example
  • Connect testable success criteria with hard constraints vs. optimization goals and predict what changes when one assumption changes
  • Verify the idea with a normal case and an important edge, failure, or scale case

Explanation

A goal like 'make the assistant better' or 'more accurate' isn't yet testable -- it must be converted into specific, checkable criteria: what does 'better' mean for THIS feature, what's the primary objective versus secondary ones, what are the hard constraints (must never do X) versus optimization goals (should generally do more of Y).

Key concepts

testable success criteriahard constraints vs. optimization goalsfailure categoriesunit of evaluation

Example

# converting a vague goal into testable criteria:
# vague: 'make the assistant more accurate'
# testable: 'the assistant must never fabricate a specific dollar amount
# (hard constraint); it should correctly resolve at least 90% of billing
# questions without escalation (optimization goal); failure categories include
# wrong amount, wrong policy citation, and unnecessary escalation'
Common mistake: Leaving a goal vague ('more accurate,' 'more helpful') without converting it into specific, checkable criteria -- an untestable goal can't actually be evaluated, only vaguely gestured at.
03Offline and Online Evaluation

What you will learn

  • Distinguish offline from online evaluation and explain why their results can genuinely differ
  • Explain how offline evaluation changes the behavior, result, or engineering decision in a concrete example
  • Connect offline evaluation with online evaluation and predict what changes when one assumption changes
  • Verify the idea with a normal case and an important edge, failure, or scale case

Explanation

Offline evaluation runs against a fixed dataset before deployment -- controlled and repeatable, but it can't fully capture real user behavior. Online evaluation observes real production traffic -- user feedback, A/B testing, and canary evaluation conceptually compare real outcomes, but labels may be immediate, delayed, or sometimes never available at all.

Key concepts

offline evaluationonline evaluationdelayed/unavailable labelsoffline-online gap

Example

# why offline success does not guarantee online success:
# offline: 'candidate scores 92% on our fixed golden dataset' -- controlled,
# repeatable, but the dataset can't capture every real user request pattern
# online: real users ask things the offline dataset never anticipated --
# offline and online evaluation answer genuinely DIFFERENT questions
Common mistake: Treating a strong offline evaluation result as proof the system will perform well in production -- offline and online evaluation answer different questions, and real user behavior can.

Key concepts

# a real, subtle risk: accidental reuse of development examples

05Adversarial, Edge-Case and Slice-Based Evaluation

What you will learn

  • Explain why aggregate scores can hide important regressions, and how slice-based evaluation surfaces them
  • Explain how aggregate-score masking changes the behavior, result, or engineering decision in a concrete example
  • Connect aggregate-score masking with slice-based evaluation and predict what changes when one assumption changes
  • Verify the idea with a normal case and an important edge, failure, or scale case

Explanation

A strong aggregate score can hide a real, important failure -- if 95% of your evaluation set is easy, routine cases, a system that fails badly on the remaining 5% (which might be your highest-stakes cases) can still show an impressive-looking overall number.

Key concepts

aggregate-score maskingslice-based evaluationadversarial casesedge cases

Example

# how an aggregate score can hide a real regression:
# 95% easy cases pass perfectly, 5% hard/critical cases fail completely
# aggregate score: 95% -- looks excellent
# per-slice score: 100% (easy), 0% (critical) -- reveals the actual, serious problem
# an aggregate-only report would have completely missed this
Common mistake: Reporting only an aggregate score without breaking results down by slice -- a strong overall number can hide a complete failure in a smaller, possibly higher-stakes subset of cases.
06Deterministic and Rule-Based Metrics

What you will learn

  • Apply deterministic metrics appropriately, and recognize their limits for open-ended output
  • Explain how exact match changes the behavior, result, or engineering decision in a concrete example
  • Connect exact match with schema/rule-based validation and predict what changes when one assumption changes
  • Verify the idea with a normal case and an important edge, failure, or scale case

Explanation

Deterministic metrics give a clear, reproducible pass/fail: exact match, normalized exact match (ignoring case/whitespace differences), regex or rule-based checks, JSON/schema validity, required-field presence, and action/tool-call validity against an allowlist.

Key concepts

exact matchschema/rule-based validationprecision/recall/F1 (task-appropriate)limits for open-ended output

Example

# where deterministic metrics genuinely work, and where they don't:
# 'is this valid JSON matching our schema?' -> deterministic check works perfectly
# 'is this a good, helpful explanation of a concept?' -> no deterministic check
# can capture this; the output is open-ended, with no single correct string
Common mistake: Applying an exact-match check to open-ended, free-form output -- a correct paraphrase or equally valid alternative phrasing will fail this check despite being a genuinely good answer.
07Semantic and Model-Based Similarity Metrics

What you will learn

  • Explain why semantic similarity to a reference answer does not guarantee factual correctness
  • Explain how semantic/embedding-based similarity changes the behavior, result, or engineering decision in a concrete example
  • Connect semantic/embedding-based similarity with reference-answer dependence and predict what changes when one assumption changes
  • Verify the idea with a normal case and an important edge, failure, or scale case

Explanation

Semantic similarity (often computed via embedding-based comparison) measures how close a response is in MEANING to a reference answer, capturing valid paraphrases that exact match would incorrectly reject.

Key concepts

semantic/embedding-based similarityreference-answer dependencefalse similaritysimilarity vs. factual correctness

Example

# why HIGH semantic similarity does not prove factual correctness:
# reference: 'the return window is 30 days'
# candidate: 'the return window is 90 days' -- HIGH semantic similarity
# (same topic, same sentence structure, same general meaning) but factually
# WRONG -- semantic similarity measures closeness in MEANING, not truth
Common mistake: Treating a high semantic-similarity score as proof of factual correctness -- a response can be very close in overall meaning to a reference while still stating a specific wrong fact.
08LLM-as-a-Judge and Its Failure Modes

What you will learn

  • Name specific, documented failure modes of automated LLM judging, and explain why judges must be audited, not trusted by default
  • Explain how LLM-as-a-judge changes the behavior, result, or engineering decision in a concrete example
  • Connect LLM-as-a-judge with answer-order sensitivity and predict what changes when one assumption changes
  • Verify the idea with a normal case and an important edge, failure, or scale case

Explanation

LLM-as-a-judge uses a model to grade another model's output against a rubric -- reference-based (comparing to a known-good answer) or reference-free, pointwise (scoring one response) or pairwise (comparing two).

Key concepts

LLM-as-a-judgeanswer-order sensitivityverbosity preferencerubric ambiguityauditing judges

Example

# a judge's number is not automatically ground truth -- it's a measurement
# subject to its own design flaws:
# answer-order sensitivity: swap which answer is shown first -> the judge's
# preference can flip, even though nothing about the ANSWERS changed
# verbosity preference: a longer, padded, factually worse answer can be
# preferred over a shorter, correct one -- length itself biasing the grade
Common mistake: Treating an automated judge's score as objective ground truth simply because it's produced by a model and expressed as a number -- judges have documented, real biases (order, verbosity, rubric ambiguity) and must be calibrated against human review, not trusted by default.
09Pairwise Comparison and Ranking

What you will learn

  • Design a counterbalanced pairwise comparison protocol and interpret win rates with appropriate uncertainty
  • Explain how pairwise comparison changes the behavior, result, or engineering decision in a concrete example
  • Connect pairwise comparison with counterbalanced ordering and predict what changes when one assumption changes
  • Verify the idea with a normal case and an important edge, failure, or scale case

Explanation

Pairwise comparison (is response A or response B better, for the same input) is often genuinely easier and more reliable than absolute scoring, since humans and judges are typically better at relative comparison than assigning a stable absolute number.

Key concepts

pairwise comparisoncounterbalanced orderingtieswin-rate uncertainty

Example

# why counterbalancing order matters for pairwise comparison:
# without counterbalancing: A is ALWAYS shown first -- if there's any position
# bias at all, it's now indistinguishable from A actually being better
# with counterbalancing: A first for half the comparisons, B first for the
# other half -- position bias, if present, cancels out rather than contaminating the result
Common mistake: Running pairwise comparisons without counterbalancing presentation order -- any position bias present becomes indistinguishable from a genuine quality difference between A and B.

11Factuality, Hallucination and Groundedness Evaluation

What you will learn

  • Precisely distinguish factual correctness, faithfulness, groundedness, and citation correctness
  • Explain how factual correctness changes the behavior, result, or engineering decision in a concrete example
  • Connect factual correctness with faithfulness and predict what changes when one assumption changes
  • Verify the idea with a normal case and an important edge, failure, or scale case

Explanation

These related-sounding terms mean genuinely different things: factual correctness asks whether a claim is true in the world; faithfulness to provided context asks whether the response accurately reflects what was actually given to the model (even if that source material itself were wrong).

Key concepts

factual correctnessfaithfulnessgroundednesscitation correctnessanswer completeness

Example

# distinct failure modes, easy to conflate:
# faithful to context but factually wrong: the source document itself was
# outdated, and the response accurately reflects that outdated document
# groundedness failure: the response states something the context never said
# at all -- fluent, plausible-sounding, but simply invented
# citation failure: cites a real source, but that source doesn't actually say what's claimed
Common mistake: Treating a fluent, confident, relevant-sounding response as evidence it's factually correct -- fluency and relevance are properties of the TEXT; factual support is a separate question requiring its own explicit check.

  • Explain how retrieval vs. generation failure changes the behavior, result, or engineering decision in a concrete example
  • Connect retrieval vs. generation failure with recall-oriented retrieval checks and predict what changes when one assumption changes
  • Verify the idea with a normal case and an important edge, failure, or scale case

Explanation

A bad RAG answer can fail for two genuinely different reasons: retrieval failure (the system didn't find the relevant document at all) or generation failure (it found the right document but the model still produced a wrong or ungrounded answer from it) -- and the fix for each is completely different.

Key concepts

retrieval vs. generation failurerecall-oriented retrieval checksanswer groundednessend-to-end RAG evaluation

Example

# why attribution matters -- the SAME bad answer, two different real causes:
# cause 1 (retrieval failure): the relevant document was never retrieved at all
# -- fixing this means improving the retrieval step (indexing, query, ranking)
# cause 2 (generation failure): the relevant document WAS retrieved correctly,
# but the model still produced an ungrounded or wrong answer from it --
# fixing THIS means improving prompting/generation, not retrieval at all
Common mistake: Treating every bad RAG answer as a generation problem (or every one as a retrieval problem) without checking which stage actually failed -- misattributing the failure means fixing the wrong.

15Continuous Evaluation in Production

What you will learn

  • Design a continuous evaluation practice that closes the loop without treating noisy signals as ground truth
  • Explain how continuous evaluation changes the behavior, result, or engineering decision in a concrete example
  • Connect continuous evaluation with delayed/unavailable labels and predict what changes when one assumption changes
  • Verify the idea with a normal case and an important edge, failure, or scale case

Explanation

Continuous evaluation means scheduled evaluation runs against the current system, safely sampling real production cases (with privacy considerations), human review queues for cases needing judgment, and dashboards tracking quality, latency, cost, and safety together over time.

Key concepts

continuous evaluationdelayed/unavailable labelsquality/latency/cost/safety dashboardsnoisy signals vs. ground truth

Example

# why user feedback alone is a limited, noisy signal for continuous evaluation:
# most users who had a fine experience never leave feedback at all
# users who DO leave feedback are not a representative sample of all users
# -- treating raw feedback volume/sentiment as ground truth risks reacting
# to a skewed, noisy signal rather than genuine system quality
Common mistake: Treating raw user feedback or a single noisy production signal as definitive ground truth about system quality -- feedback is inherently unrepresentative and delayed, and reacting to it.

Exercises

Convert a vague goal into measurable claims

Convert 'make the assistant better' into specific, testable evaluation claims.

Hint

e.g. 'the assistant must never fabricate a specific dollar figure' (hard constraint) and 'it should correctly resolve at least 90% of billing questions without escalation' (optimization goal) -- specific and checkable, not vague

Choose the evaluation unit for a multi-turn assistant

A support assistant handles multi-turn conversations. What's the appropriate unit of evaluation, and why not just individual responses?

Hint

the full conversation, since a single response might look fine in isolation while the overall conversation fails to resolve the user's actual issue

Choose offline, online, or combined evaluation

A team wants to know if a new prompt version improves customer satisfaction. Would offline evaluation alone answer this?

Hint

not alone -- offline evaluation can check quality against a fixed dataset, but real customer satisfaction requires online evaluation (A/B testing, real user feedback) since offline and online evaluation answer different questions

Build a representative golden set

Design a golden dataset for a customer-support assistant, ensuring it's representative rather than just convenient.

Hint

include a mix of real and synthetic cases across categories, difficulty levels, and user types -- not just the easy cases that happened to be readily available

Diagnose sampling bias in an easy-only set

An evaluation set contains only straightforward, easy examples. What's wrong, and what's the risk?

Hint

sampling bias -- the set isn't representative of real difficulty distribution; a system can score very well on this set while failing badly on the harder, possibly more important cases it never gets tested against

Add adversarial and edge cases

An evaluation set only contains normal, everyday requests. Add adversarial and edge cases to it.

Hint

e.g. deliberately ambiguous requests, unusually long inputs, requests designed to probe a known weak point, and requests from an edge-case user type not represented in the normal set

Identify a slice hidden by a strong aggregate score

A system shows 95% overall accuracy. What slice-level check would you run before trusting this number?

Hint

break the score down by category, difficulty, and user type -- a 95% aggregate can hide a critical slice performing far worse, invisible in the single overall number

Choose metrics for a fixed task

A task requires the model to output valid JSON matching a schema. What metric type fits, and why not semantic similarity?

Hint

schema/rule-based validation -- this is a deterministic, well-defined correctness question; semantic similarity is designed for open-ended meaning comparison, not structural validity checking

Design validation checks for model-generated JSON

Design the validation checks needed before model-generated JSON is used by a downstream system.

Hint

valid JSON parsing, schema conformance, required fields present, correct types, enum/allowlist values, and range checks where applicable

Explain semantic similarity's factuality gap

Explain why a factually wrong answer can still score highly on semantic similarity to a correct reference.

Hint

semantic similarity measures closeness in overall meaning and structure, not truth value -- a wrong number or fact stated in an otherwise similarly-structured sentence can still be very close in embedding space

Diagnose judge sensitivity

An automated judge's preference between two answers flips when their presentation order is swapped. What does this reveal, and what else might cause similar inconsistency?

Hint

answer-order sensitivity -- a documented judge bias; verbosity preference (favoring longer answers) and rubric ambiguity can cause similar inconsistency

Design a counterbalanced pairwise protocol

Design a pairwise comparison protocol that avoids position bias.

Hint

present candidate A first for half the comparisons and candidate B first for the other half, so any position bias present cancels out rather than contaminating the result

Write a human-evaluation rubric with scoring anchors

Write a rubric for rating response helpfulness on a 1-5 scale, including concrete scoring anchors.

Hint

e.g. '5 = fully resolves the question with accurate, complete detail; 3 = partially helpful but missing key information; 1 = unhelpful or misleading' -- with a worked example at each anchor

Design a disagreement and adjudication policy

Two human reviewers disagree on a response's score. Design a policy for handling this.

Hint

a defined adjudication process -- e.g. a third reviewer breaks ties, or disagreements above a certain gap are escalated to a senior reviewer -- rather than leaving disagreement unresolved or averaged blindly

Separate retrieval from generation failure

A RAG system gives a wrong answer. The retrieved document was actually correct and relevant. What's the failure category, and what should be fixed?

Hint

generation failure -- the right context was retrieved, but the model still produced a wrong or ungrounded answer from it; the fix is in prompting/generation, not retrieval

Evaluate an agent's tool choice and side effects

An agent's final answer says a task completed successfully. What specific things would you check beyond that final text?

Hint

tool selection (was the right tool used), argument correctness, sequence correctness, unnecessary tool calls, and actual side effects (what really changed in the system, not just what the agent claims)

Detect evaluation-set contamination

A model performs suspiciously well on a public benchmark compared to its performance on your own held-out cases. What might be happening?

Hint

possible contamination -- the model may have seen the benchmark data during training (memorization rather than genuine generalization); your own held-out cases are a more trustworthy signal

Design a regression and continuous-evaluation plan

Design a plan balancing quality, latency, and cost for ongoing regression testing of a production LLM feature.

Hint

a versioned golden dataset, per-slice thresholds (not just aggregate), a defined acceptable trade-off (e.g. how much latency increase is acceptable for a given quality gain), and scheduled continuous runs with alerting on regressions
